One flood can touch many policyholders at once when they rely on suppliers in the same places. This tab shows where that shared dependence sits.

An illustrative portfolio

Imagine this is the apparel-sector policy portfolio of an insurance company, and these are its six largest policyholders. This is a hypothetical example. The companies are real and their revenue comes from public reports, but no actual insurance policies, premiums, or claims are involved.

Why shared dependence matters

Accumulation is insurance shorthand for many policies being hit by the same event. Supplier networks can create accumulation that never shows up on the insured address.

Before an event

If a flood or cyclone builds over a country that several policyholders depend on, the insurer can see in advance where losses may cluster, warn those policyholders, and ask about backup suppliers. That matters most for contingent business interruption cover: payment for income lost when a supplier, not the policyholder, is shut down.

After an event

Knowing which policyholders depend on the affected country helps decide which claims to handle first, and the Sayari flags show which suppliers, payees, or vendors deserve a check before any money is paid.

How the six were chosen

  1. Start with a defined group

    Ten publicly listed apparel companies that publish audited annual reports or U.S. 10-K filings. The list was fixed on , before any revenue was compared.

  2. Use a comparable figure

    Where a company reports apparel separately from footwear or equipment (Nike, adidas), the apparel figure is used. Where a company sells mainly apparel, total revenue is used. A company that does not separate apparel is set aside.

  3. Convert to U.S. dollars

    Figures are converted at the Federal Reserve's 2025 average exchange rates, because the annual reports reviewed do not state a single rate.

  4. Keep the six largest

    The six highest-ranked companies become the illustrative portfolio. This is a ranking within the group of ten, not a global list.

Points to keep in mind

    Ownership and sanctions concerns are cheapest to act on before money moves. These flags tell an insurer where to look first.

    Flags are prompts to investigate, not findings of wrongdoing. Each flag means Sayari's records show a connection worth asking about, such as a trade link, a shared owner, or a listed product. It does not show that a supplier uses forced labor or breaks sanctions. All flags were retrieved from Sayari on ; each flag type names its underlying source.

    Counts are numbers of sampled suppliers (out of ). A supplier can carry several flag types, so rows do not add up. Supplier-level records are kept in the working files for review and are not shown on this site.

    How the map and key numbers were calculated

    • Suppliers. For each policyholder, the first 25 companies Sayari's trade records show shipping to it were reviewed. Up to eight that look like producers were kept; freight forwarders, retail stores, group companies, and individuals were removed. Result: suppliers ( of them one step further up the chain).
    • Country. Each supplier is assigned to the country named in its Sayari address, or to Sayari's country field when no address was given. Bubbles sit at the country's label point from Natural Earth, not at any supplier site.
    • Flagged share. A supplier counts as flagged if its own Sayari record carries at least one forced-labor, sanctions, or ownership flag. Flags Sayari borrows from a "possibly the same" record are not counted.
    • Flood context. GDACS flood events active between were matched to suppliers by country only.
    • Focus countries. Countries where at least three of the six policyholders have a sampled supplier and GDACS recorded at least one flood in the window.
    • Shared dependence. A policyholder "shares" a country if at least one other policyholder also has a sampled supplier there.

    What this data cannot tell you

    • Country-level is not site-level. A supplier in a country with a flood was not necessarily flooded. No site has been matched to a flooded area, because supplier coordinates and GDACS flood outlines were both unavailable.
    • Flags are not findings. They are leads from Sayari's analysis of public records and must be checked before any conclusion is drawn.
    • Coverage varies. Sayari's trade data is richer for some countries than others, and this sample covers mostly first-tier suppliers. A country with few or no suppliers here may simply be under-recorded.
    • Sample, not spend. Supplier counts reflect a small sample, not how much each policyholder buys, and no supplier in this sample is shared by two policyholders; the overlap shown is at country level.
    • Products are mixed. Sayari's apparel-only filter did not work through our connection, so some suppliers (for example Nike's) make footwear.
    • Flood only. Cyclone tracks are not yet included. GDACS alert levels are its own categories, not our risk scores. NOAA's U.S. flood service did not apply because no sampled supplier is in the United States.
    • No real insurance data. No policies, premiums, exposures, or losses are used.

    How Sayari data appears

    Sayari data appears on this site only in derived or aggregated form: counts by country, policyholder, and flag type. No supplier names, identifiers, or addresses are shown. Supplier-level records are kept privately for review and are not part of this site.

      How this could be extended

      • Pull supplier coordinates through a direct Sayari export and match sites to GDACS flood outlines, turning country context into site-level exposure.
      • Add cyclone tracks and wind fields (for example GDACS tropical-cyclone alerts) alongside floods.
      • Weight dependence by shipment volume or value from trade records instead of supplier counts.
      • Expand beyond eight suppliers per policyholder and into deeper tiers to test for genuinely shared suppliers.
      • Add historical events to compare past floods with later business-interruption patterns.
      • Test the views with underwriters and claims teams to see which decisions they actually change.
